# 1. What Is Corporate English?

Corporate English is the style of English used in professional environments such as:

- Offices
- IT companies
- Consulting firms
- Banks
- Manufacturing companies
- Startups
- Multinational companies
- Client meetings
- Project teams
- Vendor communication
- Management communication

Corporate English is not about using difficult words.

It is about being:

- Clear
- Professional
- Polite
- Concise
- Confident
- Structured
- Action-oriented

### Casual English

> Send me the file quickly.

### Better corporate English

> Could you please share the file by 3:00 PM today?

The second version is better because it is polite, clear, and includes a deadline.

# 2. How to Use This Handbook

Do not try to memorize everything in one day.

Use the handbook in four stages.

## Stage 1 — Understand

Read the explanation.

## Stage 2 — Observe

Study the examples.

## Stage 3 — Practice

Create your own sentences using the same pattern.

## Stage 4 — Apply

Use the sentence in real workplace conversations, emails, or meetings.

A useful learning formula is:

> **Learn → Repeat → Use → Review**

# 3. Corporate Communication Mindset

Before learning sentences, understand how professionals communicate.

A good corporate message usually answers:

1. Why are you contacting the person?
2. What is the current situation?
3. What action is required?
4. Who needs to act?
5. When is the action required?

## Example

Weak:

> Server issue. Please check.

Better:

> The production server has been unavailable since 10:15 AM, and users are unable to access the application. Could the infrastructure team please investigate this on priority and share an update by 11:00 AM?

The improved version gives:

- Problem
- Impact
- Owner
- Priority
- Expected response time

# 4. Essential Grammar for Corporate English

You do not need perfect academic grammar to communicate professionally.

However, several grammar topics are extremely important.

---

## 4.1 Subject-Verb Agreement

The verb should match the subject.

### Correct

> The report **is** ready.

> The reports **are** ready.

### Incorrect

> The reports is ready.

---

## 4.2 Present Simple

Use the present simple for:

- Regular activities
- Processes
- Responsibilities
- Facts

Examples:

> I manage the invoice processing module.

> The system sends a reminder every morning.

> Finance reviews the request before approval.

---

## 4.3 Present Continuous

Use it for activities happening now or temporarily.

> We are investigating the issue.

> The development team is working on the fix.

> I am preparing the monthly report.

---

## 4.4 Present Perfect

Use present perfect when an action is completed but still relevant now.

Structure:

> have/has + past participle

Examples:

> We have completed the deployment.

> The vendor has submitted the invoice.

> I have shared the document with the team.

Compare:

> I shared the document yesterday.

This uses simple past because the exact past time is mentioned.

---

## 4.5 Simple Past

Use it for completed actions in the past.

> We deployed the change yesterday.

> The issue occurred after the patch.

> I spoke with the vendor this morning.

---

## 4.6 Future Forms

### Will

Use for decisions, promises, or predictions.

> I will share the report by evening.

### Going to

Use for plans.

> We are going to migrate the application next month.

### Present Continuous for Future

> We are meeting the client tomorrow.

---

## 4.7 Modal Verbs

Modal verbs are extremely useful in professional communication.

### Can

> Can you share the file?

### Could

More polite:

> Could you please share the file?

### Would

> Would you be available for a quick discussion?

### May

> May I add one point?

### Should

> We should verify the data before deployment.

### Must

Stronger requirement:

> All production changes must be approved.

### Might

Possibility:

> The issue might be related to the database connection.

---

## 4.8 Articles: A, An, The

### A

Used for one non-specific thing.

> We need a developer.

### An

Before a vowel sound.

> We received an invoice.

### The

Used when the listener knows which item you mean.

> The invoice has been approved.

---

## 4.9 Prepositions

Common workplace combinations:

| Expression | Correct Usage |
|---|---|
| responsible for | I am responsible for the module. |
| interested in | I am interested in automation. |
| involved in | She is involved in the migration. |
| depend on | The release depends on approval. |
| discuss with | I will discuss this with Finance. |
| update on | Please share an update on the issue. |
| access to | I need access to the server. |

Incorrect:

> discuss about the issue

Correct:

> discuss the issue

or

> have a discussion about the issue

---

## 4.10 Countable and Uncountable Nouns

Correct:

> information

Not:

> informations

Correct:

> advice

Not:

> advices

Correct examples:

> Please share the information.

> I need some advice.

> We received three requests.

---

## 4.11 Conditional Sentences

### First Conditional

Real future possibility.

> If the testing is completed today, we will deploy tomorrow.

### Second Conditional

Hypothetical situation.

> If we had more resources, we could finish earlier.

### Third Conditional

Past hypothetical.

> If we had tested the integration earlier, we would have detected the issue.

---

## 4.12 Passive Voice

Passive voice is common in corporate English because the action is sometimes more important than the person.

Active:

> The team deployed the application.

Passive:

> The application was deployed successfully.

Use passive voice when:

- The actor is not important.
- The actor is unknown.
- You want neutral wording.

# 5. Professional Vocabulary

Corporate vocabulary should make communication clearer, not more complicated.

---

## 5.1 Common Workplace Words

### Action Item

A task assigned during a meeting.

> My action item is to prepare the deployment plan.

### Deadline

The final time by which something must be completed.

> The deadline is Friday.

### Deliverable

An output that must be provided.

> The technical document is one of the project deliverables.

### Dependency

Something required before another activity can proceed.

> The production release depends on security approval.

### Blocker

A problem preventing progress.

> Access permission is currently a blocker.

### Stakeholder

A person or group affected by or involved in a project.

> We should update all key stakeholders.

### Alignment

Agreement or common understanding.

> Let us align on the scope before starting development.

### Escalation

Raising an issue to a higher authority because normal resolution is not working.

> The issue has been escalated to management.

### Turnaround Time / TAT

The amount of time required to complete something.

> The expected turnaround time is two business days.

### ETA

Estimated Time of Arrival or completion.

> What is the ETA for the fix?

### FYI

For Your Information.

> FYI, the deployment has been completed.

### EOD

End of Day.

> Please share the report by EOD.

### COB

Close of Business.

> We expect approval by COB Friday.

### ASAP

As Soon As Possible.

Use carefully because it can sound vague or demanding.

Better:

> Please share this by 4:00 PM today.

# 6. Sentence Building for the Workplace

A useful professional sentence structure is:

> **Context + Action + Deadline**

Example:

> Since the client demo is scheduled for tomorrow, please complete the testing by 5:00 PM today.

Another pattern:

> **Situation + Impact + Request**

Example:

> The API is currently unavailable, which is blocking invoice processing. Could you please check the service and share an update?

---

# 7. Formal vs Informal English

## Informal

> Hey, send that file.

## Professional

> Hi Rahul, could you please share the file when you get a chance?

## More formal

> Dear Rahul, could you please share the requested file by 2:00 PM today?

Choose the level based on:

- Relationship
- Seniority
- Communication channel
- Urgency
- Company culture

# 8. Tone in Corporate Communication

Tone is how your message sounds emotionally.

The same information can sound:

- Friendly
- Aggressive
- Defensive
- Neutral
- Helpful
- Respectful

### Aggressive

> You still haven't fixed this.

### Neutral

> The issue appears to be unresolved. Could you please share the current status?

### Collaborative

> It looks like the issue is still open. Please let me know if you need any input from our side to move this forward.

---

# 9. Polite and Diplomatic English

Diplomatic communication reduces unnecessary conflict.

---

## 9.1 Instead of Giving Orders

Instead of:

> Send the report.

Use:

> Could you please share the report?

---

## 9.2 Instead of Saying "You Are Wrong"

Use:

> I may have a different understanding.

> I believe there may be another way to look at this.

> Based on the data I have, the result appears to be different.

---

## 9.3 Instead of Saying "This Is Not My Job"

Use:

> This activity is currently handled by the Finance team. I can connect you with the appropriate contact.

---

## 9.4 Instead of Saying "I Don't Know"

Use:

> I don't have that information at the moment, but I can check and get back to you.

---

## 9.5 Instead of Saying "No"

Use:

> I may not be able to complete this today, but I can deliver it by tomorrow afternoon.

---

# 10. Speaking Confidently at Work

Professional confidence does not mean speaking quickly.

It means:

- Speaking clearly
- Using short sentences
- Organizing thoughts
- Avoiding unnecessary filler words
- Giving complete answers

A useful structure is:

> **Point → Reason → Example → Next step**

Example:

> I recommend postponing the deployment. The testing is not yet complete, and we found two critical issues this morning. For example, invoice posting is failing for one vendor category. I suggest we complete testing today and deploy tomorrow.

---

# 11. Pronunciation and Fluency

Fluency is more important than copying a particular accent.

Focus on:

- Clear pronunciation
- Correct stress
- Pausing
- Natural rhythm
- Avoiding speaking too fast

## Useful Practice Method

Read one corporate email aloud every day.

Record yourself saying:

> Good morning everyone. I will give a quick update on the project. Development is complete, testing is in progress, and we are currently targeting Friday for deployment.

Listen for:

- Unclear words
- Long pauses
- Repeated fillers
- Speed

### Common filler words

- Umm
- Actually
- Basically
- Like
- You know
- So

Using them occasionally is normal.

Using them in every sentence makes communication less confident.

Safe topics include:

- Weather
- Travel
- Food
- Weekend
- Work events
- Sports
- Conferences
- General interests

# 14. Email Writing Mastery

A professional email normally contains:

1. Subject
2. Greeting
3. Purpose
4. Context
5. Required action
6. Deadline if needed
7. Closing

---

## 14.1 Good Subject Lines

Weak:

> Issue

Better:

> Production Login Issue – Support Required

Weak:

> Update

Better:

> Weekly Project Status – Invoice Automation – 12 Aug

---

## 14.2 Email Opening Phrases

> I am writing regarding...

> This is regarding...

> I would like to request...

> Please find below...

> As discussed...

> Further to our conversation...

> Thank you for your email.

---

## 14.3 Request Email

**Subject:** Request for Production Server Access

> Hi Team,  
>
> I would like to request production server access for the deployment scheduled on 14 August.  
>
> Please provide access to the following server for my user ID before 3:00 PM tomorrow.  
>
> Kindly let me know if any approval is required from my side.  
>
> Regards,  
> Alex

---

## 14.4 Status Update Email

**Subject:** Project Status Update – Invoice Automation

> Hi Team,  
>
> Please find the current status below:
>
> - Development: Completed
> - Unit Testing: Completed
> - UAT: In Progress
> - Production Deployment: Planned for Friday
>
> The only open dependency is business approval for the latest UAT build.
>
> Regards,  
> Alex

---

## 14.5 Delay Email

> Hi Team,  
>
> We would like to inform you that the planned deployment will be delayed by one day due to an issue identified during final testing.  
>
> The team is working on the fix, and the revised deployment target is Thursday evening.  
>
> We will share another update after validation is completed.  
>
> Regards,  
> Alex

---

## 14.6 Escalation Email

A professional escalation should focus on the issue, not blame.

> Hi Team,  
>
> We are currently blocked due to the pending firewall approval requested on 8 August.  
>
> This is impacting the production deployment scheduled for tomorrow.  
>
> Could you please help prioritize the approval or advise on the expected completion time?  
>
> Regards,  
> Alex

---

## 14.7 Thank-You Email

> Thank you for your quick support in resolving the issue. The application is now working as expected.

---

## 14.8 Email Closing Phrases

Formal:

> Regards,

> Kind regards,

> Best regards,

Friendly professional:

> Thanks,

> Thank you,

# 17. Meeting English

Meetings require specific language for different situations.

---

## 17.1 Starting a Meeting

> Good morning, everyone. Thank you for joining.

> Let's get started.

> The objective of today's meeting is to finalize the deployment plan.

---

## 17.2 Explaining the Agenda

> We have three items on the agenda today.

> First, we will review the current status. Then, we will discuss the open issues. Finally, we will agree on the next steps.

---

## 17.3 Giving an Update

> Development is complete, and testing is currently in progress.

---

## 17.4 Asking for an Opinion

> Rahul, what are your thoughts on this approach?

> Does anyone see any risk with this plan?

---

## 17.5 Entering a Discussion

> May I add one point?

> If I may, I'd like to share one observation.

---

## 17.6 Interrupting Politely

> Sorry to interrupt, but may I clarify one point?

---

## 17.7 Disagreeing

> I understand your point, but I have a slightly different view.

---

## 17.8 Returning to the Topic

> Coming back to the original point...

> Let's return to the main question.

---

## 17.9 Closing the Meeting

> To summarize, we have agreed on three action items.

> Thank you, everyone. I will share the meeting notes shortly.

# 19. Status Updates and Progress Reporting

A strong status update includes:

- Completed
- In progress
- Pending
- Risks
- Blockers
- Next steps

Example:

> **Completed:** API development and unit testing  
> **In Progress:** UAT validation  
> **Pending:** Business sign-off  
> **Blocker:** None  
> **Next Step:** Production deployment after approval

Verbal version:

> Development and unit testing are complete. UAT is currently in progress, and we are waiting for business sign-off. There are no technical blockers at the moment. Once approval is received, we will proceed with production deployment.

---

# 20. Explaining Technical Issues

Use this structure:

> **Problem → Impact → Cause → Action → ETA**

Example:

> The invoice upload service is currently failing for PDF files. As a result, users cannot submit new invoices. Initial analysis suggests that the issue is related to the file-processing service. The development team is working on a fix, and we expect to complete validation by 4:00 PM.

Avoid overly technical language with business users.

Technical:

> The API returned HTTP 500 because the JSON deserialization layer encountered a null object reference.

Business-friendly:

> The system encountered an internal processing error while reading the submitted data.

---

# 21. Asking Questions Professionally

Instead of:

> What do you want?

Use:

> Could you please clarify the expected output?

Instead of:

> Why did you do this?

Use:

> Could you help me understand the reason for this change?

Instead of:

> You didn't tell me.

Use:

> I may have missed the earlier communication. Could you please share the details again?

---

# 22. Giving Instructions

Good instructions should be:

- Clear
- Sequential
- Specific
- Easy to verify

Example:

> Please follow these steps:
>
> 1. Log in to the application.
> 2. Open the Invoice Processing module.
> 3. Select the pending invoice.
> 4. Click Approve.
> 5. Confirm that the status changes to "Approved."

# 23. Requesting Help and Support

Useful phrases:

> Could you please help me with this issue?

> I would appreciate your support on this.

> Could you please guide me on the next steps?

> We need your assistance to complete the deployment.

> Please let me know if you need any information from my side.

# 24. Following Up

Following up is normal in corporate communication.

### First follow-up

> Hi Rahul, just following up on the access request shared yesterday. Could you please let me know the current status?

### Second follow-up

> Hi Rahul, this is a gentle follow-up regarding the pending access request. The delay may impact tomorrow's deployment. Could you please help prioritize this?

Avoid:

> Why are you not replying?

---

# 25. Reminders

Professional reminder:

> This is a gentle reminder that the UAT sign-off is due today.

Alternative:

> Just a quick reminder regarding the pending approval.

For a deadline:

> Kindly share your confirmation by 4:00 PM today so that we can proceed with the scheduled deployment.

---

# 26. Apologizing Professionally

A strong apology should include:

1. Acknowledgment
2. Responsibility when appropriate
3. Correction
4. Prevention

Example:

> Apologies for the delay in sharing the report. The data validation took longer than expected. The report is now attached, and I have updated the process to avoid similar delays in future.

Avoid over-apologizing.

You do not need to say "sorry" in every message.

---

# 27. Giving and Receiving Feedback

## 27.1 Giving Positive Feedback

> You handled the client discussion very well, especially when explaining the technical issue in simple language.

## 27.2 Constructive Feedback

Use:

> Observation + Impact + Suggestion

Example:

> I noticed that the status report did not include the open blockers. This made it difficult for stakeholders to understand the deployment risk. Going forward, please include a blocker section in the report.

## 27.3 Receiving Feedback

> Thank you for the feedback. I understand the point and will incorporate it in the next report.

---

# 28. Disagreement and Conflict Management

Professional disagreement should focus on ideas, not people.

Avoid:

> That makes no sense.

Use:

> I have some concerns about that approach.

Avoid:

> You're wrong.

Use:

> I believe the data may suggest a different conclusion.

Avoid:

> We already told you.

Use:

> This was also discussed in the previous meeting, but I can summarize the agreed approach again.

---

# 29. Escalation Communication

Escalation should be factual.

Include:

- Issue
- Date raised
- Current status
- Impact
- Help required

Example:

> The server access request was raised on Monday and remains pending. The production deployment is scheduled for tomorrow, so the delay is now a project risk. We would appreciate your support in getting the approval completed today.

Do not use escalation as a threat.

---

# 30. Negotiation English

Negotiation means finding an acceptable solution.

Useful phrases:

> What flexibility do we have on the timeline?

> Would it be possible to complete the first phase by Friday and the remaining work next week?

> If we prioritize this task, we may need to postpone the other activity.

> Could we explore an alternative approach?

> That timeline may be difficult for us. Would Monday be acceptable?

---

# 31. Client and Customer Communication

Client communication should be:

- Clear
- Respectful
- Solution-focused
- Free from internal blame

Avoid:

> Our infrastructure team didn't configure the server correctly.

Better:

> We identified a configuration issue in the environment and are working with the relevant team to resolve it.

## Handling a complaint

> Thank you for bringing this to our attention. We understand the impact this has caused. The team is investigating the issue on priority, and we will share the next update by 2:00 PM.

---

# 32. Leadership and Management English

Managers often need language for:

- Delegation
- Motivation
- Prioritization
- Feedback
- Decision-making
- Conflict resolution

### Delegation

> Rahul, could you take ownership of the testing activity and share the final status by Thursday?

### Prioritization

> Please treat the production issue as the top priority today.

### Motivation

> The team has made good progress. Let's focus on closing the remaining two issues before the release.

### Decision

> Based on the current risk, we will postpone the deployment until testing is complete.

---

# 33. Project Management English

Common project terms:

### Scope

The work included in the project.

> This request is currently outside the approved project scope.

### Milestone

A major checkpoint.

> UAT completion is our next milestone.

### Risk

A possible future problem.

> Vendor dependency is a project risk.

### Issue

A problem that has already occurred.

> The API failure is a current issue.

### Assumption

Something believed to be true for planning.

> Our assumption is that the business team will be available for UAT.

### Change Request

A formal request to modify scope or requirements.

> This enhancement will require a change request.

### Resource

A person, system, budget, or capability needed for work.

> We need one additional developer for the integration activity.

# 35. Numbers, Dates, Time, and Data

Professional communication requires precise numbers.

Instead of:

> Sales increased a lot.

Use:

> Sales increased by 18% compared with the previous quarter.

Instead of:

> It happened last week.

Use:

> The issue was first reported on 7 August.

## Reading numbers

₹1,50,000 in Indian numbering:

> one lakh fifty thousand rupees

International style:

> one hundred fifty thousand rupees

## Percentages

25%:

> twenty-five percent

0.5%:

> zero point five percent

## Dates

Corporate-friendly formats:

> 12 August 2026

or

> August 12, 2026

Avoid ambiguous dates like:

> 08/09/2026

This could mean 8 September or August 9 depending on country.

# 36. Writing Reports and Documents

Professional writing should follow:

> **Purpose → Facts → Analysis → Recommendation → Action**

## Example Executive Summary

> The invoice automation project is currently in UAT. Core invoice extraction and validation functions are working as expected. Two issues remain open related to vendor-specific layouts. We recommend completing the fixes and regression testing before moving to production.

## Writing Principles

Use:

- Short paragraphs
- Headings
- Bullets
- Tables
- Specific actions
- Measurable results

Avoid:

- Very long sentences
- Repetition
- Emotional language
- Unnecessary technical jargon

# 37. Interview and Career English

## 37.1 Tell Me About Yourself

Structure:

> Present role → Experience → Key skills → Achievements → Career goal

Example:

> I am a full-stack software engineer with seven years of experience in enterprise application development. I have worked on Angular, PHP, .NET, databases, and workflow automation. In my current role, I manage business applications related to invoice processing and internal operations. I am now looking for opportunities where I can work on larger-scale systems and take more ownership of solution design.

## 37.2 Explaining a Project

Use STAR:

- Situation
- Task
- Action
- Result

Example:

> Our invoice process required significant manual effort. I was responsible for improving the automation. I redesigned the validation workflow and integrated OCR-based extraction. As a result, the team reduced manual review effort and improved processing speed.

## 37.3 Strengths

> One of my strengths is problem-solving. I am comfortable analyzing production issues, identifying the root cause, and coordinating with multiple teams to implement a fix.

## 37.4 Weakness

Do not give a fake weakness.

Better:

> Earlier, I sometimes spent too much time trying to perfect technical details. I have improved this by prioritizing business impact and delivery timelines.

# 38. Cross-Cultural Corporate Communication

Multinational workplaces include people with different communication styles.

Some cultures communicate directly.

Others prefer indirect and diplomatic language.

Good global communication should be:

- Simple
- Neutral
- Respectful
- Free from slang
- Easy to understand

Avoid regional expressions that international colleagues may not understand.

Example:

Instead of:

> Do the needful.

Use:

> Please review the document and share your approval.

Instead of:

> Revert back.

Use:

> Please reply.

or

> Please get back to me.

# 39. Common Corporate Idioms and Expressions

Use corporate idioms carefully, especially with international teams.

### Touch Base

Meaning: Have a short discussion.

> Let's touch base tomorrow.

### On the Same Page

Meaning: Have the same understanding.

> Let's make sure we are on the same page regarding the scope.

### Move the Needle

Meaning: Create meaningful progress.

> We need to focus on actions that will move the needle.

### Circle Back

Meaning: Return to a topic later.

> Let's circle back to this after the client meeting.

### Take Offline

Meaning: Discuss separately outside the current meeting.

> We can take this discussion offline.

### Deep Dive

Meaning: Detailed analysis.

> We need a deeper dive into the error logs.

### Low-Hanging Fruit

Meaning: Easy improvements.

> Let's start with the low-hanging fruit before redesigning the entire system.

Do not overuse corporate jargon.

# 48. Final Learning Strategy

Corporate English mastery does not come from memorizing difficult words.

It comes from repeatedly practicing common workplace situations.

Use this cycle:

1. Learn one communication pattern.
2. Read five examples.
3. Write five original examples.
4. Speak them aloud.
5. Use one at work.
6. Review mistakes.
7. Repeat.

The most important rule is:

> **Be clear before trying to sound impressive.**

A simple sentence that everyone understands is better than a complicated sentence filled with unnecessary corporate jargon.

# Bonus Section C — Advanced Executive Communication

When speaking with senior management, avoid giving unnecessary technical detail.

Use:

> **Outcome → Risk → Decision Required**

Example:

> The project is 90% complete and remains on track for Friday. The only open risk is pending security approval. We need management support to obtain the approval by Thursday afternoon.

Avoid:

> We completed API method number 15, changed two database queries, fixed CSS, and now we are waiting for the firewall request.

Senior leaders often need the business implication first.

# Bonus Section G — Five Levels of Politeness

Request:

### Level 1 — Direct

> Send the report.

### Level 2 — Neutral

> Please send the report.

### Level 3 — Polite

> Could you please send the report?

### Level 4 — Diplomatic

> Could you please share the report when convenient?

### Level 5 — Polite + Deadline

> Could you please share the report by 3:00 PM today so that we can complete the review?

For most professional situations, Levels 3–5 are best.

# Final Principle

> **Professional English = Clear thinking expressed in clear language.**

Your goal is not to sound complicated.

Your goal is to make it easy for the other person to understand:

- Why it matters
- What needs to happen next

- When it must be completed
